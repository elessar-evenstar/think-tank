Placement help

Position the headband across your forehead with the ear pieces resting behind your ears. Keep hair away from the sensors and make sure the forehead and behind-ear sensors touch your skin. Adjust gently for a snug, comfortable fit.

Lab 1: Blink Explorer

This short session tests how often ThinkTank detects a blink in response to each of ten prompts. Watch your live TP9 and TP10 signals for brief downward spikes, which may be caused by blinks. At the end, you will see how many prompted trials had a detection, how many did not, and how many extra or unprompted detections occurred.

Blink Explorer Results

A prompted trial counts as detected when a detection occurs within 1.6 seconds of the cue. Extra detections and detections outside that window count as unexpected.

These results compare detections with prompts, not independently verified blinks. Natural blinks can also appear as unexpected detections.

Calculation details

Each accepted window contains 512 samples (about two seconds) from AF7 and AF8. Each channel is mean-centered and Hann-windowed before calculating frequency power. Matching powers are then averaged across channels. Values use the existing algorithm's squared-voltage normalization, not a calibrated power spectral density. The display smooths updates; the vertical range is fixed after the first accepted window. Values beyond that range are clipped and reported.

Technical details

EEG is shown within +/-600 microvolts of each channel's baseline mean. Motion shows angular speed from 0 to 150 degrees per second. Values outside these ranges are clipped at the graph edges; the graphs do not automatically zoom. Timing is approximate and based on packet arrival. Each movement graph covers the full prompted recording, not automatically detected movement boundaries.

ThinkTank

What is ThinkTank?

ThinkTank is an interactive aquarium controlled by a Muse 2 headband. It uses EEG frequency features for an EEG engagement estimate, blink artifacts in the electrode recordings to detect blinks, and separate motion sensors to track head movement.

Tank and Lab

Tank connects your Muse signals to the aquarium: blinks activate bubbles, the EEG engagement estimate changes fish speed, and head movements adjust the view.

Lab keeps receiving and processing signals while holding the aquarium controls and stats at their current settings. The aquarium keeps animating. Switch back to Tank at any time to gradually resume live control without reconnecting.

Blink Explorer compares ten prompted blinks with detector responses and shows the TP9 and TP10 signals. Artifact Explorer compares saved still and head-movement recordings so you can explore movement artifacts at your own pace.

The Science Behind It

Neurons communicate through electrical and chemical signals. Incoming signals open ion channels, changing the voltage across a neuron's membrane. Excitatory inputs can make the inside less negative; an action potential occurs only if the neuron reaches its firing threshold.

Scalp EEG mainly reflects the combined currents associated with incoming signals, called postsynaptic potentials, in many similarly oriented cortical neurons active together. It does not directly count individual action potentials. These currents produce tiny voltage differences that reach the scalp.

Electrodes are conductive sensors. Muse measures voltage at AF7 and AF8 on the forehead and TP9 and TP10 behind the ears relative to a reference electrode. It amplifies these small signals and converts them into digital samples. The recordings also contain eye, muscle, movement, and contact artifacts.

How Blink Detection Works

ThinkTank looks for short downward deflections in TP9 and TP10 to detect blinks. These are eye-related artifacts mixed into the EEG recording, rather than a neural measure of engagement. Other artifacts can sometimes trigger the detector too.

To look for the downward spike, the algorithm compares the newest 14 measurements with the 96 measurements immediately before them. It detects a blink when both channels fall at least 35 signal units below their recent baseline, their average drop is at least 70 units, and the drop is at least 2.8 times larger than the normal background noise. A very large spike reaching -400 units in either channel also counts.

After a blink is detected, the five bubble fountains in the aquarium fade in together. A short cooldown prevents one blink from being counted several times.

EEG Engagement Estimate

ThinkTank calculates its EEG engagement estimate from AF7 and AF8, examining about two seconds of data from each channel separately. It calculates each channel's theta, alpha, and beta power, then averages the matching band powers. This preserves activity even when one channel's wave peaks as the other dips.

EEG contains fluctuations at different frequencies, measured in hertz (Hz), or cycles per second. The algorithm measures power, or signal strength, in theta (4-7 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz), and beta (13-30 Hz). These bands can relate to several brain processes; none uniquely measures attention or relaxation.

It calculates beta power / (alpha power + theta power), then maps ratios from 0.2 to 2.6 onto a 0-100 scale. Larger relative beta power raises the score. Large signal amplitudes, detected blinks, or head movement pause the estimate at its last valid value until a clean two-second window is available. The stats show the reason for the pause; smoothing makes valid changes gradual.

A score of 70 does not mean 70% focused. This experimental EEG engagement estimate controls fish speed; it is not a validated measure of attention. Eye and muscle activity or poor electrode contact can still affect it despite the adjustments.

How Head Movement Works

Head movement comes from Muse's motion sensors, separately from EEG. The gyroscope measures rotation speed. A detected left turn widens the camera's field of view to zoom out; a detected right turn narrows it to zoom in.

The accelerometer estimates head tilt from gravity. ThinkTank uses changes in this estimate to adjust target radius: how far the point the camera looks at lies from the aquarium's center. Detected upward movement increases this radius; downward movement decreases it.

With the current camera position, increasing target radius makes the view less steeply downward; decreasing it makes the view more steeply downward. The camera stays in place and does not directly copy your head angle. Movement above a threshold gradually changes the view, which settles when you hold still. Both responses are smoothed.
